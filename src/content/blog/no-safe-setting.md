In Mauldin, Officer Ellie Anna Hammond [ran a former partner's license plate through Flock's camera network 166 times](https://www.fitsnews.com/2026/08/10/mauldin-police-officer-used-flock-to-track-ex-lover-166-times-separation-papers-say/) over 4 months, logging each search as a welfare check, a warrant check, or a traffic infraction. None of it was true. Mauldin fired Hammond in August, one of [at least 8 South Carolina law-enforcement employees fired across 6 agencies](https://www.fitsnews.com/2026/08/31/flock-abuse-scandals-more-s-c-officers-terminated/) since June for using Flock to track people in their personal lives.

Those firings look like accountability, and in a narrow way they are. But every one of them ran the same course: someone broke an internal rule for an obviously personal reason, and months later they got flagged for it. The system stopped no one in the moment and warned none of the people being tracked. The officers who get fired are the ones who broke a rule, and the machine does its quietest, largest damage when nobody breaks one at all.

For years, [federal agents ran plate readers outside gun shows](https://www.americas1stfreedom.org/content/wsj-federal-agents-are-scanning-license-plates-of-gun-show-attendees/), logging everyone who pulled in to buy a firearm and checking them against government records. And last year [a sheriff's deputy in Texas ran one Flock search across 83,000 cameras](https://www.404media.co/a-texas-cop-searched-license-plate-cameras-nationwide-for-a-woman-who-got-an-abortion/) to hunt down a woman after a tip that she'd had an abortion. One search went after a woman exercising one right; the other built a record of people exercising another. Nobody broke a rule in either case. The tools did exactly what they were built to do. Mass surveillance does its gravest harm when it works exactly as intended.

This is what we keep getting wrong about systems like Flock. We argue about the settings: how long the data is kept, what reason an officer types, who gets access, how wide the sharing runs, etc. But a system that [photographs every car](/blog/99-percent-of-the-plates-they-scan), stores where it went, and lets thousands of agencies (and even [people outside law enforcement](https://www.404media.co/city-learns-flock-accessed-cameras-in-childrens-gymnastics-room-as-a-sales-pitch-demo-renews-contract-anyway/) in some cases) search that record is a standing power to find anyone, and that kind of standing power is dangerous, regardless of what regulations are supposedly in place. There's NO safe setting, because the danger is the record itself, sitting there, one login or one policy change or one breach away from being turned on you.

Time and again, surveillance systems have been turned on the people that law enforcement and elected officials claimed they were supposed to protect, and Flock is only the latest. The NSA, one of the most vetted and audited workforces on earth, [logged 12 confirmed cases](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna53118897) of analysts using its systems to spy on people they were romantically interested in. In 1993, [federal auditors called the FBI's national crime database "extremely vulnerable to misuse, particularly by individuals with authorized access"](https://www.gao.gov/products/t-ggd-93-41) and asked Congress for a law with real penalties; no law came, and the recommendation still sits marked "closed, not implemented."

An [Associated Press count found more than 325 officers and staff](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/police-sometimes-misuse-confidential-work-databases-for-personal-gain-ap/) fired, suspended, or forced out for misusing confidential databases in a single 3-year span. Give people a searchable record of other people's lives, and some number of them will predictably reach into it for reasons that are anything but professional.

This is the case regardless of the technology. Point the cameras at [faces instead of plates](/blog/flock-safety-patent-facial-recognition) and you get [Robert Williams, arrested in front of his wife and daughters](https://www.aclu.org/cases/williams-v-city-of-detroit-face-recognition-false-arrest) in Detroit on a false facial-recognition match, and Porcha Woodruff, 8 months pregnant, jailed for a carjacking she had nothing to do with. [At least 14 Americans have been wrongly arrested this way](https://www.aclu.org/news/privacy-technology/more-than-a-dozen-wrongful-arrests-due-to-police-reliance-on-facial-recognition-technology) that we know of. And the biggest name in that business, Clearview AI, built its tool by [scraping tens of billions of faces off the open internet without consent](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/ryanmac/clearview-ai-local-police-facial-recognition), sold it to thousands of police departments, and watched officers run it on protesters and their own friends. Once a private company can assemble that from the open web and sell it to anyone, the capability has slipped past even the thin oversight a government has to answer to.

Point it at phones and you get [Zachary McCoy, a man in Florida who became a burglary suspect](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/google-tracked-his-bike-ride-past-burglarized-home-made-him-n1151761) because a fitness app logged his bike ride past the wrong house. When the [Supreme Court ruled this June](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/06/court-rules-that-law-enforcements-use-of-geofence-warrant-was-a-search/) that police can't run those dragnet "geofence" searches without a warrant, it left [a simpler workaround standing](/blog/the-4th-amendment-loophole): federal agencies skip the judge and buy Americans' location data from brokers instead, a loophole the [stalled Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2024/04/fourth-amendment-not-sale-act-passed-house-now-it-should-pass-senate) was written to close. Different source, same result.

Notice that all of this was explicitly legal. Williams, Woodruff, McCoy, the woman in Texas: the systems worked exactly as built, and that's exactly what caused harm.

That's the problem with the reform answer, the one that says regulate these systems rather than refuse them. Reforms govern how the capability gets used, and the danger is that the capability exists. A warrant rule decides who reaches into the record; it doesn't un-build the record. An audit describes the abuse after it has already happened. Shorter retention narrows the window without closing it. Grant every one of these, and the searchable record is still there, open to abuse.

The safeguards on offer are also weaker than they look, and [Flock has spent the last year proving it](/blog/flock-safetys-track-record). It made a case number mandatory for searches only after [a Washington Post investigation this summer](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/08/19/we-found-cops-who-misused-flock-their-police-departments-didnt-know/), even though that number is never checked to make sure it's real. It points to its audit trail as the answer to misuse, then in December [quietly blacked out the officer names, the searched plates, and the stated reasons](https://footnote4a.org/news/secret-searches-part2) in those very logs, the moment journalists started digging into them. When a vendor can add a safeguard under pressure and strip it away at will, the safeguard is nothing but a marketing decision.

Which leaves one honest conclusion. If you can't build the capability without building the danger, and you can't regulate the danger away, then the move is to stop building these systems entirely and take down the ones already standing. That sounds radical right? Local lawmakers don't seem to think so. [Greer suspended its entire Flock program for 90 days](https://www.postandcourier.com/greenville/news/greer-south-carolina-flock-cameras-suspended/article_63442586-c542-45cd-9d4e-5fbf02fc6e74.html). [Oconee County just voted to get mass surveillance off county property](https://www.foxcarolina.com/2026/09/02/oconee-county-advances-ordinance-restricting-flock-cameras/) and pull its own cameras out of the network. [The legislative goal](/blog/h4675-strongest-alpr-bill-in-sc) follows the same logic: fewer of these systems, and no new ones.

We keep a [running map](/) of every Flock camera we can find in South Carolina, county by county. If you'd rather your county take them down than add more, tell your representatives that you want these surveillance systems gone.
